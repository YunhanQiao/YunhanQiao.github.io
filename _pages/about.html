About

GenAI assistants and coding agents can now write and change code for us, but someone still has to understand it.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Computer Science at Oregon State University, advised by Dr. Christopher Hundhausen in the SEAL Lab. My research lies at the intersection of Software Engineering and Human–Computer Interaction.

I study how developers work with GenAI coding assistants and agents, such as GitHub Copilot and Cursor, on unfamiliar, existing codebases (so-called brownfield tasks): how they communicate with AI, how they verify and integrate AI-generated code, and whether faster, more correct work also means deeper code comprehension. Through controlled experiments, screen-recorded behavioral analysis, prompt analysis, and interviews, I derive design implications for future AI coding agents and developer tools. My work appears in ACM TOCE, ICER, and IEEE AIxSE.

Before Oregon State, I earned my M.S. in Software Engineering from Arizona State University, working with Dr. Robert LiKamWa.

Research

GenAI is changing how software gets built and what developers need to understand. My work is organized around three connected questions.

01

Perform vs. Understand

When Copilot makes developers faster and more correct in unfamiliar code, does their understanding keep up, and which behaviors separate those who understand from those who don't?

02

Say, Do, Understand

How do developers communicate with coding agents: what they write, what they do while the agent works, and what they can explain afterwards?

03

Design for Understanding

How should AI coding tools be designed so developers actively verify, explain, and stay in control of AI-generated code, rather than accepting it without understanding?

arXiv preprint · 2026Under Review

Beyond the Prompt: Linking What Developers Ask, Do, and Understand with Coding Agents

Yunhan Qiao, Summit Haque, Christopher Hundhausen

We present Say, Do, Understand, an end-to-end workflow for analyzing what developers write to a coding agent, what they do while it works, and what they can explain afterwards. It has five stages (Capture, Prepare, Analyze, Integrate, Interpret) and three instruments: a prompt codebook, a coding scheme for screen-recorded activity, and rubrics for explaining the process and the solution. Applied to ten experienced developers using GitHub Copilot on an unfamiliar codebase, it produced four personas; for two developers, task performance and understanding split apart.

arXiv preprint · 2026Under Review

Faster but Not Wiser: GitHub Copilot Decouples Programming Performance from Code Comprehension in Brownfield Tasks

Yunhan Qiao, Md Istiak Hossain Shihab, Summit Haque, Christopher Hundhausen

In a within-subjects study, 15 CS graduate students implemented features in an unfamiliar codebase with and without Copilot. Copilot significantly improved performance, but overall comprehension did not improve (p = 0.59), and performance gains were not associated with comprehension gains. Higher-comprehension participants engaged in verification loops, performing write-then-view transitions 4.7× more often (p = 0.001). Task success alone is not a reliable signal of code comprehension, which calls for GenAI tools that support active verification of AI-generated code.

ACM TOCE 26(2) · 2026Published

A Systematic Literature Review of the Use of GenAI Assistants for Code Comprehension: Implications for Computing Education Research and Practice

Yunhan Qiao, Md Istiak Hossain Shihab, Christopher Hundhausen

Selected by the TOCE editorial board for presentation at SIGCSE TS 2027

We review 31 studies (2022–2024) that use GenAI to support code comprehension. We classify the approaches and tools, the methods used to study them, and the evidence for their effectiveness. GenAI assistants often give inaccurate or unclear explanations, and novices struggle to write effective prompts. We discuss implications for research and practice and outline directions for future work.
